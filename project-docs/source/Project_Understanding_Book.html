1 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1.1 Project Overview & Core Mission

MerchantShield AI is an end-to-end, defensive transaction-risk decisioning platform engineered for electronic commerce merchants and digital payment service providers. Developed for the Razorpay AI Buildathon 2026 (Track 02 — AI Risk Manager), the system solves a fundamental, systemic failure in modern payment security: the conflation of raw machine learning statistical classification with operational decision-making.

In a single evaluation cycle, MerchantShield AI ingests an incoming payment transaction along with its customer's prior transaction history, computes 15 strictly-prior behavioral features without future temporal leakage, estimates the probability of fraud using a frozen LightGBM gradient-boosted decision tree classifier (candidate_lgbm_v1.pkl), mathematically validates and decomposes that estimate using grounded Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and maps the probability to a bounded, auditable operational recommendation (ALLOW, ALLOW_WITH_MONITORING, STEP_UP_VERIFICATION, or BLOCK) using a transparent five-rule deterministic policy table. Crucially, the system is strictly advisory: it never executes irreversible financial side effects such as freezing bank accounts, moving funds, or issuing unilateral chargebacks.

Core Architectural Axiom: The Three-Tier Separation of Concerns
MerchantShield AI enforces a structural, architectural decoupling between three distinct operational concerns:
  1. The Statistical Estimator: A machine learning model estimates the objective probability of fraud: P(fraud) ∈ [0.0, 1.0]. It does not decide what to do.
  2. The Human Presentation Layer: A deterministic function maps the continuous probability to a 0–100 risk score and categorical tier (LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH, CRITICAL) solely for human cognitive ergonomics.
  3. The Governance Policy Engine: An independent, deterministic rule cascade evaluates (probability, amount) against an asymmetric economic cost threshold (0.40) to select a bounded operational action.
Because these tiers are strictly decoupled, modifying display buckets can never silently alter financial actions, and bugs in explanatory text can never alter transaction disposition.

1.2 Target Users & Integrating Systems

MerchantShield AI is designed to integrate into two distinct operational environments:

  • Payment Gateways & Merchant Acquirers (e.g., Razorpay, Stripe, Adyen): Placed inline within the pre-authorization or post-authorization payment routing pipeline. The gateway supplies the transaction context and receives a bounded risk recommendation in sub-50 milliseconds.
  • Merchant Risk Operations Teams & Fraud Analysts: Interacting through the dedicated React 19 dashboard, fraud analysts investigate high-risk transactions, review grounded SHAP attribution bars explaining exactly why an alert fired, verify historical velocity, and inspect an immutable SQLite audit trail.

1.3 Real-World Problem & Core Capabilities

Conventional fraud detection models routinely fail rigorous production scrutiny for three compounding reasons:

  1. Deceptive Metric Reporting on Imbalanced Classes: At real-world fraud rates (1% to 3.5%), reporting standard classification "accuracy" is completely meaningless (a naive model predicting 100% legitimate achieves 98.5% accuracy while catching 0% of fraud).
  2. The Arbitrary 0.5 Threshold Flaw: Defaulting to a 0.50 classification cutoff ignores the severe economic asymmetry between the cost of a false positive (customer checkout friction, support tickets, lost lifetime value) and the cost of a false negative (chargeback fees, stolen merchandise, merchant penalties).
  3. The Black-Box Explanatory Deficit: Complex ensemble models or neural networks cannot explain to a human compliance officer or merchant why a transaction was halted, leading to regulatory vulnerability and merchant distrust.

MerchantShield AI addresses all three problems simultaneously through verified capabilities:

  • Leakage-Safe Feature Pipeline: All 15 behavioral signals (velocity windows, amount z-scores, rolling failure rates, device novelty) are computed strictly from events prior to the current transaction timestamp, proven by a 213,000-row leakage verification test suite.
  • Cost-Driven Threshold Optimization: Operating threshold (0.40) was selected by sweeping thresholds against an explicit financial cost model (₹50 flat FP cost; 0.5 × amount FN cost) on a held-out validation set, subject to an operational floor of ≥80% fraud recall.
  • Grounded SHAP Explainability with Additivity Verification: Per-transaction feature contributions derived from shap.TreeExplainer verify mathematical additivity (base_value + Σshap ≈ predict_proba within 1e-4) on every API invocation.
  • V2 Production Hardening: Defensive model validation rejects non-finite probabilities (NaN/Inf) and out-of-range floats, while feature bounds checking guards against pathological input spikes. Historical signal quality grading (MINIMAL, LIMITED, MODERATE, ESTABLISHED) transparently communicates context sufficiency.

2 PROBLEM STATEMENT

2.1 The Existing Situation: Fraud Detection in Practice

In contemporary electronic commerce and digital payments, merchant enterprises face a relentless operational challenge: discerning legitimate consumer transactions from unauthorized or fraudulent activity in real time. Before modern intelligent risk platforms exist, organizations typically rely on one of two extremes:

  1. Static Rule Engines: Primitive "if-then" heuristic rules defined by risk committees (e.g., "flag any transaction exceeding ₹10,000" or "block any payment originating from a new device at night"). While transparent and fast, static rules are brittle, incapable of learning nuanced customer behaviors, easily circumvented by organized fraud rings, and notorious for generating massive false-positive rates that insult loyal customers.
  2. Opaque Monolithic ML Classifiers: Second-generation systems employ black-box statistical algorithms (random forests, neural networks, or gradient-boosted trees) that output a raw score between 0.0 and 1.0. However, these systems are almost universally deployed with naive defaults (such as a 0.50 classification cutoff) and lack transparent operational interpretation.

When a fraudulent transaction bypasses these systems, the merchant suffers a chargeback: the true cardholder discovers the unauthorized charge, contacts their issuing bank, and claws back the funds. The merchant loses the merchandise shipped, loses the transaction revenue, incurs a punitive bank chargeback fee (typically ₹1,000 to ₹2,500 per incident), and risks being terminated by payment aggregators if their monthly chargeback ratio breaches 1.0%. Conversely, when a legitimate customer is wrongly declined due to an aggressive heuristic rule (a false positive), the customer experiences embarrassment, abandons their cart, and often migrates permanently to a competing platform.

2.2 The Three Fundamental Failures of Conventional Systems

As documented in the repository's core problem analysis, fraud detection systems frequently collapse under rigorous architectural and operational scrutiny due to three compounding systemic failures:

Failure 1: Meaningless Metric Reporting Under Extreme Class Imbalance

In real-world payment streams, fraudulent transactions account for a tiny fraction of total volume—typically between 1.0% and 3.5%. Under this extreme class imbalance, traditional machine learning metrics such as raw classification accuracy or standard ROC-AUC become dangerously misleading.

Consider an evaluation dataset containing 100,000 transactions where 1,500 are fraudulent (1.5% fraud prevalence). A trivial, degenerate classifier that predicts legitimate for every single transaction achieves an impressive 98.5% accuracy, yet it allows ₹10,000,000 in fraud losses and catches zero criminal activity. Similarly, ROC-AUC evaluates the true positive rate against the false positive rate across all negative samples; because the pool of legitimate transactions is vast (98,500), tens of thousands of false alarms barely register on the ROC curve, yielding an deceptively high ROC-AUC (e.g., 0.95) even when nine out of ten flagged transactions are completely innocent customers.

Failure 2: The Arbitrary 0.50 Threshold Without Economic Justification

Standard machine learning textbooks and off-the-shelf library defaults (such as scikit-learn's predict()) apply a symmetric probability threshold of p ≥ 0.50 to convert a probability into a binary decision. In financial risk management, this default is economically irrational.

Fraud risk is inherently asymmetric in cost:

  • The Cost of a False Positive (FP): Wrongly challenging or declining a legitimate customer incurs an operational cost (customer service inquiries, manual verification labor, and customer goodwill friction). In MerchantShield's model, this is modeled as an assumed flat operational expense of ₹50 per occurrence.
  • The Cost of a False Negative (FN): Permitting a fraudulent transaction causes direct financial loss. The merchant is liable for the stolen amount and chargeback penalties. In MerchantShield's cost model, this is formulated as a configurable fraction (e.g., 50% to 100%) of the actual transaction amount. On a ₹50,000 purchase, a false negative costs ₹25,000 to ₹50,000—between 500 and 1,000 times greater than the cost of a false positive.

Under asymmetric costs, the economically optimal threshold is almost never 0.50. Setting the threshold arbitrarily high (e.g., to maximize the harmonic F1-score at 0.80) yields high precision but misses millions in mid-range fraud, resulting in a 38% higher total financial cost to the merchant.

Failure 3: The Black-Box Explanatory Deficit

When a traditional machine learning model outputs a risk score of 0.72, it provides zero actionable reasoning. A merchant risk analyst looking at the dashboard cannot answer:

  • "Did the score jump because the customer is in a new city?"
  • "Is it because they placed five orders in the last ten minutes?"
  • "Or is it simply because they purchased an expensive television?"

Without grounded explainability, human reviewers cannot distinguish a true fraud ring attack from a loyal customer traveling on vacation. Furthermore, emerging data protection regulations and consumer credit acts strictly require financial institutions to explain adverse actions taken against consumers.

2.3 The Problem MerchantShield AI Solves

MerchantShield AI was architected from the ground up to solve these three compounding failures in a unified, auditable software platform. The central objective of the project is to provide a calibrated, economically rational, and human-explainable decisioning layer that sits between raw payment telemetry and business action.

Specifically, MerchantShield AI guarantees:

  1. Precision-Recall & Cost-Driven Evaluation: Performance is reported exclusively using Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PR-AUC), precision, recall, and total expected financial cost, measured strictly on held-out chronological test sets.
  2. Cost-Optimal Operating Threshold Selection: Rather than assuming 0.50, the platform sweeps thresholds across a dedicated validation split against a rigorous financial cost objective: 
    Total Expected Cost = (Count(FP) × Cost_FP) + Σ (Amount_FN × Fraction_FN)
    The operating cutoff is frozen at the exact mathematical minimum (0.40) subject to a hard operational requirement of ≥80% fraud recall.
  3. Mathematically Grounded Explainability: Every scored transaction receives an attribution breakdown derived from exact tree-based Shapley values, verified by a mathematical additivity check on every API call.
  4. Deterministic, Auditable Action Policy: A transparent five-rule cascade maps validated probabilities into four bounded operational recommendations (ALLOW, ALLOW_WITH_MONITORING, STEP_UP_VERIFICATION, BLOCK).

3.1 A Familiar Real-World Situation: Ananya's Handcrafted Silk Boutique

Imagine you have a close friend named Ananya who runs a growing, independent online boutique in Bangalore called "Varnam Silks". Ananya curates authentic Kanjivaram silk sarees and hand-woven garments. Over three years, Ananya has built a loyal community of several thousand repeat customers across India.

Her typical customer spends between ₹1,500 and ₹4,000 per purchase. One such loyal customer is Vikram, a software engineer living in Indiranagar, Bangalore. Over the past eighteen months, Vikram has bought five sarees from Varnam Silks for his mother and sister. Every single time, Vikram ordered from his personal iPhone while connected to his home Wi-Fi network in Bangalore, paid around ₹2,500 using his familiar HDFC credit card, and the deliveries arrived without incident.

To Vikram and Ananya, buying online feels instantaneous: Vikram clicks "Pay Now", a green checkmark appears three seconds later, Ananya packages the saree, and Blue Dart delivers it two days later.

3.2 The Nightmare Without MerchantShield: The Chargeback Trap

Now, let's see what happens when criminal fraud enters the picture without an intelligent system like MerchantShield AI.

One Tuesday evening at 1:45 AM, an unauthorized third party operating from a computer in an unfamiliar location gains access to Vikram's stored payment credentials through an unrelated data breach. The attacker visits Ananya's website, creates a cart with four of her most luxurious wedding silk sarees totaling ₹45,000 (twenty times Vikram's normal spending), and clicks "Pay Now".

What happens under a conventional, naive system?

  1. The Bank Sees Valid Card Details: Because the card number, expiration date, and CVV match the bank's database, the payment processor authorizes the charge.
  2. Ananya Ships the Goods: The next morning, Ananya sees a ₹45,000 order—her biggest sale of the week. Delighted, she carefully packages the exquisite hand-woven sarees and ships them via express courier.
  3. The True Cardholder Discovers the Theft: Three days later, the real Vikram checks his credit card statement, panics, and immediately calls his bank to report fraud: "I was asleep on Tuesday night; I never bought ₹45,000 worth of wedding sarees!"
  4. The Devastating Chargeback: The issuing bank investigates, confirms the theft, and forcibly claws back the ₹45,000 from Ananya's business bank account. On top of that, the payment processor penalizes Ananya with a ₹1,500 chargeback fee.

Ananya is left completely devastated: she has lost the ₹45,000 in revenue, her physical silk sarees are gone forever, and she had to pay a penalty fee.

In response, many merchants try to protect themselves by hiring human staff to manually review every large transaction or by installing crude, rigid rules like: "Block any order over ₹10,000". But this creates the opposite disaster: when Vikram genuinely wants to buy an expensive wedding anniversary gift for his wife for ₹25,000, the blunt rule blocks his card. Vikram is insulted, abandons the website, and buys from a giant retail competitor instead.

3.3 Introducing MerchantShield AI: The Invisible Risk Manager

This is why Ananya needs MerchantShield AI.

Think of MerchantShield AI as an extraordinarily sharp, calm, and fair chief risk officer who stands behind the cash register 24 hours a day. Every time a customer clicks "Pay", this risk manager examines the transaction in less than fifty milliseconds.

Crucially, this digital risk manager does not guess, does not jump to wild conclusions, and does not hold an axe to instantly cancel orders. Instead, it follows a disciplined three-step reasoning process:

  1. It Looks at Behavioral Memory: It immediately retrieves Vikram's past shopping habits. It notices: "Vikram usually spends ₹2,500, shops between 6 PM and 9 PM, and always uses his iPhone in Bangalore."
  2. It Estimates the Objective Probability of Fraud: It compares the current purchase against thousands of past historical patterns using a sophisticated mathematical model. It calculates: "The probability that this 1:45 AM, ₹45,000 purchase from a brand-new device in an unfamiliar region is fraudulent is 99.9%."
  3. It Applies a Balanced Business Policy: Instead of doing something irreversible, it looks at its rulebook. Because the risk is overwhelming (above 80%), it recommends: BLOCK. But if the risk had been moderate (say, Vikram buying a ₹5,000 saree while traveling on business), it wouldn't block him—it would simply recommend STEP-UP VERIFICATION, prompting Vikram for a quick One-Time Password (OTP) on his registered mobile phone!

3.4 The Complete Scenario Walkthrough: Step-by-Step

Let's trace exactly what happens under MerchantShield AI during that Tuesday night attack:

Step 1: The Trigger

At 01:45:12 UTC, the attacker clicks "Complete Purchase" on Varnam Silks' checkout page for transaction txn_live_test_001. The online storefront's backend captures the checkout event.

Step 2: Information Entering the System

The storefront packages the transaction details into a structured digital envelope:

  • Current Purchase: Amount ₹45,000; Customer ID cust_00042 (Vikram); Device ID dev_BRAND_NEW; Region region_19 (unfamiliar); Time: 1:45 AM.
  • Prior History: Vikram's past six legitimate orders from the last six months (all card payments between ₹500 and ₹2,500 from dev_known in region_03).

Step 3: What the System Does With the Information

MerchantShield AI's backend receives the envelope. In less than 15 milliseconds, its feature engine calculates 15 behavioral indicators strictly comparing the new order against the past history:

  • Amount Spike: The current amount (₹45,000) is 85.7 times larger than Vikram's historical average amount (₹525).
  • Z-Score Outlier: The amount is more than 20 standard deviations above his typical spending distribution.
  • Device Novelty: The device hardware fingerprint has never been seen before in Vikram's account history.
  • Geographic Novelty: The IP address and cellular location originate from a state where Vikram has never transacted.
  • Time of Day: The purchase is taking place in the middle of the night (overnight hours).

Step 4: How It Makes Decisions (The Probability and Policy Ladder)

The 15 numbers are passed to the trained LightGBM machine learning model. The model outputs a fraud probability of 0.9997 (99.97%).

Next, the system passes (probability=0.9997, amount=45000) to its transparent 5-rule policy table:

  • Rule 1 checks: Is probability ≥ 0.80? YES.
  • The rule fires immediately: CRITICAL_BLOCK.
  • Recommended action: BLOCK.

Step 5: The Output Produced

MerchantShield AI generates a comprehensive, transparent report:

  • Action: BLOCK
  • Risk Score: 100 / 100 (CRITICAL)
  • Grounded Explanation: "Why this transaction was flagged: Transaction amount is 85.7x the customer's historical average amount, which increased risk; Device has not been seen previously for this customer, which increased risk; Geographic location is new for this customer, which increased risk."

Step 6: What Happens Next

Because the recommendation is BLOCK, the payment gateway halts the transaction before money moves. The attacker is stopped cold. Simultaneously, Ananya's dashboard logs the incident in its audit trail, and an automated SMS is sent to the real Vikram: "Did you just attempt a ₹45,000 purchase? If not, please secure your card." Ananya ships zero goods, loses zero rupees, and avoids a ₹1,500 bank penalty.

Step 7: When Something Goes Wrong (Fail-Safe Behavior)

What happens if the database disk is full or the explanation module encounters a rare glitch?

MerchantShield AI is built with defensive isolation: the policy decision is calculated before the explanation is built and before the database is touched. Even if the SQLite audit log fails to save to disk, the API still returns the correct BLOCK decision to the checkout page, logging an internal warning (audit_persisted: false). The store's security is never compromised by an ancillary bookkeeping glitch!

The feature engineering engine in ml/features/build_features.py is built upon NumPy and pandas. While pandas handles high-level customer partitioning via groupby("customer_id"), raw NumPy arrays are used to calculate rolling statistics without incurring pandas indexing overhead. Specifically:

  • np.cumsum() and squared cumulative sums are concatenated with a leading 0.0 and sliced with [:-1] to compute running means and running standard deviations strictly on transactions prior to the current row index.
  • np.divide(..., where=prior_count > 0) ensures division-by-zero errors are mathematically trapped, outputting safe 0.0 defaults for first-time customers.
  • Vectorized timestamp integer arrays (datetime64[s]) compute trailing 5-minute, 30-minute, and 60-minute transaction velocities via pointer-sliding two-pointer loops in $O(N)$ time per customer.

4.3 LightGBM & SHAP: The Dual Reasoning Engine

LightGBM 4.7.0: High-Speed, Leaf-Wise Gradient Boosting

LightGBM was selected over Random Forest and Deep Neural Networks as the primary production classifier (ml/models/candidate_lgbm_v1.pkl). LightGBM utilizes histogram-based algorithms and leaf-wise (best-first) tree growth rather than depth-wise growth, resulting in superior accuracy on non-linear tabular data with significantly faster training and inference times (under 5 milliseconds for a batch of 1,000 transactions).

The Early Stopping Metric Bug & Its Fix (Phase 4 Engineering Record)
During candidate model training, an insidious machine learning bug was uncovered: using eval_metric="average_precision" for early stopping caused LightGBM to prematurely halt training after iteration 1, resulting in probabilities capped near 0.35 and 0% recall.

Root Cause Analysis: LightGBM's binary objective automatically evaluates binary_logloss alongside any custom metric. Under severe class imbalance (~1.5% fraud), our high scale_pos_weight=73.2 artificially inflates predicted-positive confidence, causing binary_logloss to deteriorate immediately—even while PR-AUC is rapidly climbing. By default, LightGBM requires all metrics to improve, so logloss vetoed training.

The Fix: Configured eval_metric="auc" with callbacks=[lgb.early_stopping(stopping_rounds=50, first_metric_only=True)]. This decoupled early stopping from secondary metrics, allowing boosting to converge naturally at its optimum.

SHAP 0.52.0: Exact Shapley Attributions with Additivity Checks

Rather than relying on sampling approximations (such as KernelSHAP or LIME), MerchantShield AI deploys shap.TreeExplainer. TreeExplainer leverages the tree structure of LightGBM to calculate exact Shapley values in polynomial time $O(TLD^2)$.

In ml/evaluation/explainability.py, SHAP decomposes the model's raw margin (log-odds) output: 

margin = base_value + Σ shap_values[i]
P(fraud) = 1.0 / (1.0 + exp(-margin))

Every single call to RiskExplainer.explain() mathematically reconstructs the probability and asserts: 

abs(reconstructed_prob - raw_prob) < 1e-4

If this additivity check fails, the system immediately flags additivity_check_passed=false, preventing corrupt or mismatched model explanations from reaching compliance audits.

5.4.1 backend/main.py: Lifespan & Degradation Handling

The main entry point manages the lifecycle of the FastAPI process. The lifespan context manager initializes expensive objects exactly once: 

# backend/main.py (lines 32-50)
@asynccontextmanager
async def lifespan(app: FastAPI):
    try:
        app.state.model_bundle = load_model_bundle(settings.MODEL_PATH, settings.MODEL_METADATA_PATH)
        logger.info(f"Model loaded from {{settings.MODEL_PATH}}")
    except ModelUnavailableError as e:
        # Do not crash the process -- come up degraded so /health reports it clearly
        logger.error(f"MODEL FAILED TO LOAD: {{e}}")
        app.state.model_bundle = None

    try:
        app.state.audit_store = AuditStore(settings.AUDIT_DB_URL, settings.AUDIT_DB_DIR)
        logger.info(f"Audit store ready at {{settings.AUDIT_DB_PATH}}")
    except Exception as e:
        logger.error(f"AUDIT STORE FAILED TO INITIALIZE: {{e}}")
        app.state.audit_store = None
    yield

Architectural Rationale: Rather than allowing an unhandled exception to crash the Python container during startup (which results in opaque Docker crash loops), attaching model_bundle = None allows the HTTP process to start. Orchestration systems (Kubernetes, AWS ECS) can query /health, receive status: "degraded", model_loaded: false, and handle alerting gracefully, while /risk/evaluate returns an explicit HTTP 503 Service Unavailable.

5.4.2 backend/schemas/transaction.py: Strict Prior History Validation

Pydantic model validators guarantee that callers cannot accidentally pass corrupt data or future transactions as historical context: 

# backend/schemas/transaction.py (lines 78-100)
@model_validator(mode="after")
def _prior_transactions_must_be_valid_context(self):
    txn = self.transaction
    for i, prior in enumerate(self.prior_transactions):
        if prior.customer_id != txn.customer_id:
            raise ValueError(
                f"prior_transactions[{{i}}].customer_id does not match transaction.customer_id"
            )
        if prior.timestamp >= txn.timestamp:
            raise ValueError(
                f"prior_transactions[{{i}}].timestamp ({{prior.timestamp}}) is not strictly "
                f"before transaction.timestamp ({{txn.timestamp}}) -- leaks future data!"
            )
        if prior.transaction_id == txn.transaction_id:
            raise ValueError(f"Transaction cannot be its own prior context.")
    return self

Architectural Rationale: If a payment gateway mistakenly included the transaction currently being evaluated inside the prior_transactions array, the rolling velocity and z-score calculations would double-count the transaction and leak its existence into its own baseline. This validator rejects such requests with HTTP 422 before the feature pipeline executes.

5.4.3 ml/features/build_features.py: Strictly-Prior Vectorized Statistics

To prevent data leakage, cumulative sums are shifted by one index so that a transaction at index i only sees transactions 0 through i-1: 

# ml/features/build_features.py (lines 36-48)
prior_count = np.arange(n)  # 0 for first transaction
amounts = g["amount"].values

# expanding mean/std computed strictly on values BEFORE current index
cum_sum = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(amounts)[:-1]))
cum_sum_sq = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(amounts ** 2)[:-1]))

prior_mean = np.divide(cum_sum, prior_count, out=np.zeros(n), where=prior_count > 0)
prior_var = np.divide(cum_sum_sq, prior_count, out=np.zeros(n), where=prior_count > 0) - prior_mean ** 2
prior_var = np.clip(prior_var, a_min=0, a_max=None)
prior_std = np.sqrt(prior_var)

# Require at least 3 prior transactions before computing statistical z-score
amount_zscore = np.where(
    prior_count >= 3,
    (amounts - prior_mean) / np.where(prior_std > 1e-6, prior_std, 1.0),
    0.0
)

Architectural Rationale: The z-score quantifies how anomalous the current purchase amount is relative to the customer's typical spending. Requiring prior_count >= 3 avoids calculating volatile standard deviations on sample sizes of one or two purchases, outputting a safe neutral 0.0 until an established pattern emerges.

5.4.4 ml/evaluation/policy.py: The 5-Rule Cascade

6 ARCHITECTURE, DATABASE DESIGN, AND API DESIGN

6.1 Conceptual Architecture & Component Responsibilities

MerchantShield AI operates on a modern, four-tier architecture designed to maintain strict boundaries between statistical estimation, display presentation, operational governance, and client rendering.

The system components and their isolated responsibilities are:

  • Presentation Tier (React 19 / Vite): Renders the web dashboard. Consumes the backend API purely via asynchronous JSON HTTP requests. It contains no business logic, no policy rules, and zero Python dependencies.
  • API & Validation Gateway (FastAPI / Pydantic v2): Enforces strict structural boundaries on incoming requests. Validates data types, rejects negative amounts or blank strings, and guarantees that historical context transactions belong to the same customer and possess timestamps strictly prior to the current transaction.
  • Feature Engineering & ML Intelligence (NumPy / LightGBM / SHAP): Marshals raw transaction event streams into 15 strictly-prior behavioral features, executes gradient-boosted tree inference using a frozen model artifact (candidate_lgbm_v1.pkl), and decomposes model log-odds into exact Shapley values.
  • Governance & Policy Tier (Decision Engine / SQLite): Evaluates an ordered five-rule cascade to map (probability, amount) to bounded action recommendations, recording the immutable decision facts into an isolated SQLite audit log.

Endpoint Isolation & Failure Recovery Rationale

Why does the system provide three separate POST endpoints?

  • /risk/score and /risk/explain are explicitly designed for interactive exploration, high-throughput batch simulation, and what-if analysis. They never write to the audit log, ensuring test simulations do not pollute legal compliance records.
  • /risk/evaluate is the single authoritative entry point for operational decisions. It executes the full decision engine and attempts an audit write.
The Audit-Write Failure Isolation Pattern
In production, high-throughput disk write locks or full storage partitions could cause SQLite to throw an operational exception. Under a naive implementation, this exception would cause /risk/evaluate to return an HTTP 500 Internal Server Error, leaving the merchant payment gateway blind and forcing the merchant to either drop the sale or let unvetted fraud pass through!

MerchantShield AI handles this through Audit Isolation in backend/api/routes.py: 
audit_persisted = False
audit_error = None
if audit_store is not None:
    try:
        audit_store.record_decision(...)
        audit_persisted = True
    except AuditPersistenceError as e:
        audit_error = str(e)
If audit persistence fails, the API still returns the valid HTTP 200 risk decision (with audit_persisted: false and audit_error populated). The merchant receives their fraud decision, and the audit write failure is surfaced as an operational warning rather than crashing the transaction.

6.4 Architectural Trade-Offs, Invariants & Decoupling

Figure 6.2: The Five-Rule Deterministic Policy Decision Cascade (Source: ml/evaluation/policy.py).

The 5-Rule Policy Cascade (First Match Wins)

The decision engine implements a strict priority ladder. In ml/evaluation/policy.py, rules fire in order:

  1. CRITICAL_BLOCK (p ≥ 0.80): Recommends BLOCK. At 0.80 probability, validation precision is 94.1%—the model is overwhelmingly confident that the transaction is criminal.
  2. HIGH_STEP_UP (p ≥ 0.40): Recommends STEP_UP_VERIFICATION. This operates at the cost-optimal decision threshold (0.40). It catches 88.2% of fraud while subjecting borderline transactions to low-friction verification (e.g., SMS OTP or biometric challenge) rather than an outright decline.
  3. MEDIUM_AMOUNT_ESCALATION (p ≥ 0.15 AND amount ≥ ₹25,000): Recommends STEP_UP_VERIFICATION. Even though model probability is moderate, the high transaction value creates severe financial downside if fraudulent, warranting step-up friction.
  4. MEDIUM_MONITOR (p ≥ 0.15): Recommends ALLOW_WITH_MONITORING. Elevated risk above baseline, but low enough that customer checkout friction is unwarranted.
  5. LOW_ALLOW (Catch-all): Recommends ALLOW. Clean transaction with minimal risk signals; permitted with zero friction.

8.1 The Project in One Complete Narrative

MerchantShield AI is an explainable, defensive transaction-risk decisioning system developed for the Razorpay AI Buildathon 2026. The platform bridges the dangerous gap between statistical machine learning models and operational business action by cleanly decoupling three fundamental concerns:

  1. Statistical Probability Estimation: A frozen LightGBM classifier estimates P(fraud) ∈ [0.0, 1.0] from 15 strictly-prior behavioral features computed across customer transaction sequences without future leakage.
  2. Mathematical Explainability: A SHAP TreeExplainer decomposes the model's raw margin output into exact feature attributions, verifying additivity (base + Σshap ≈ predict_proba) on every evaluation.

8.5 Self-Testing Examination & Grounded Answers

Q1: Why is reporting raw classification accuracy dangerous in fraud detection?

Answer: Because fraud prevalence is extremely low (1–3%). A trivial model predicting 100% legitimate achieves 98.5% accuracy while catching zero fraud. Metrics must report PR-AUC, precision, recall, and total financial cost.

Q2: Why was the operating threshold set to 0.40 rather than the default 0.50 or the F1-maximum 0.80?

Answer: Fraud losses are asymmetric: false negatives (stolen merchandise) cost orders of magnitude more than false positives (₹50 operational check). Sweeping thresholds on validation data against the cost model identified 0.40 as the exact mathematical minimum cost subject to maintaining ≥80% recall. The F1-maximum (0.80) weights precision and recall equally, which misses mid-range fraud and increases total costs by ~38%.

Q3: How does MerchantShield AI prove that its feature engineering has zero data leakage?

Answer: In ml/features/build_features.py, cumulative sums are shifted by one index (np.cumsum(...)[:-1]) so that row i only observes rows 0 through i-1. Automated tests in tests/test_no_leakage.py verify that feature values match the historical sequence exactly when re-computed incrementally.

Q4: Why are SHAP explanations structurally prevented from influencing the decision engine's action?

Answer: SHAP explains the model's internal reasoning, which can exhibit feature correlation or credit-splitting. Financial actions require hard, auditable guarantees. The function signature of evaluate_policy(probability, amount) physically cannot receive SHAP explanations, ensuring an explanation bug can never silently alter an operational action.

Q5: Why does a transaction with probability 0.25 show as risk category "LOW" while receiving an action of "ALLOW_WITH_MONITORING"?

Answer: Because the 0–100 risk score buckets (0–30 is LOW) are informational presentation tools for human cognitive ergonomics, whereas the action policy enforces a 0.15 floor for elevated monitoring. Decoupling display buckets from action rules ensures presentation adjustments never accidentally alter financial actions.

Q6: What happened during Experiment A when the frozen synthetic model was tested on real IEEE-CIS data?

Answer: The frozen model failed with an inverted ROC-AUC of 0.443. The root cause was domain distribution shift: on IEEE-CIS data, cards with thousands of prior transactions produced extreme z-scores that the synthetic model had never encountered during training, inverting the probability ranking.

Q7: How did Phase 15 improve fraud detection on real IEEE-CIS chargeback data?

Answer: Phase 15 introduced the card_product_share feature (fraction of a card's prior transactions in the current product category). This emerged as the 3rd most important feature by gain, reducing false positives by 17.7% and expected cost by 17.0%, elevating test ROC-AUC to 0.771.

Q8: What is the Audit-Write Failure Isolation pattern in backend/api/routes.py?

Answer: If an operational error prevents SQLite from persisting the audit record, the exception is caught and logged. The API still returns the valid HTTP 200 risk decision with audit_persisted: false, ensuring ancillary logging failures never block checkout transactions.

Q9: How does the system handle "cold-start" transactions where a customer has zero prior history?

Answer: Neutral sentinel values are assigned (time_since_prev_txn_min = 99999.0, z-scores = 0.0), history-dependent features are filtered out of the SHAP narrative, and the API returns signal_quality: {{ level: "MINIMAL" }} to transparently alert analysts.

Q10: Why are customer IDs and device IDs omitted from the SQLite audit log table?

Answer: To adhere to privacy-by-design standards. The audit log exists solely to prove what the risk decisioning system evaluated and why, avoiding the unnecessary and legally hazardous duplication of merchant customer PII.

8.7 Engineering Glossary & Final Revision Checklist

Technical Glossary

  • Class Imbalance: An extreme disparity between positive (fraud) and negative (legitimate) examples, invalidating raw accuracy.
  • Temporal Data Leakage: An error where information from future events inadvertently contaminates feature calculations for past transactions.
  • Operating Threshold: The mathematical probability cutoff above which an alert or friction action is triggered.
  • Asymmetric Cost Function: A loss formulation where false negatives and false positives carry unequal financial weights.
  • Shapley Value: A game-theoretic measure calculating the fair marginal contribution of each feature to a model's prediction.
  • Additivity Property: The mathematical requirement that the base expected value plus the sum of all feature attributions equals the model's prediction.
  • Bounded Action: An operational recommendation that is reversible and non-destructive, avoiding automated unilateral financial transfers.
  • Signal Quality: An operational rating (MINIMAL to ESTABLISHED) indicating the volume of historical context available for behavioral inference.
